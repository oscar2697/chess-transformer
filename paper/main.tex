\title{Searchless Chess Transformer: Attention-Based Policy and Value Learning}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}We investigate whether a pure Transformer, trained only on high-ELO games and deprived of any explicit search, can predict strong chess moves (policy) and evaluate positions (value) through attention alone. On a 570k-position elite dataset, a masked cross-entropy objective lifts top-1 move prediction from chance level to 15.5\% (top-3 36\%) on held-out positions. The value head, in contrast, is characterized as a constant-predictor collapse: under game-result targets, loss reweighting ($\times$3) and head-specific LR ($\times$5) leave the MSE at the label-variance floor; under engine-evaluated targets (Lichess public evaluations DB), the MSE drops to 0.038 but the predicted-value standard deviation is 0.000 and Pearson correlation with Stockfish is 0.0 --- the model simply outputs the target mean. We show why MSE alone is misleading for value heads and identify gradient competition with the policy objective as the likely cause. All experiments were orchestrated by a five-agent LLM pipeline whose decisions are logged for reproducibility. We release the full pipeline as an open research scaffold for searchless chess.\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Inspired by the DeepMind view that rich representations can be learned without hand-crafted heuristics~\cite{Silver2017_1712_01815}, we ask how far a searchless Transformer can go in classical chess: no MCTS, no alpha-beta, only attention over a FEN token sequence feeding policy and value heads. This work contributes (i) a from-scratch policy/value Transformer evaluated with legal-move masking, (ii) an analysis of what legal-move masking contributes over naive cross-entropy, (iii) a CLS-attention interpretability protocol for tactical motifs (RQ3), and (iv) an LLM graph-agent pipeline that makes every experimental decision auditable. We deliberately report intermediate, honest results: the goal is to document the trajectory of a research scaffold, not to claim playing strength.
\section{Related Work}
\cite{Vaswani2017_1706_03762} Attention Is All You Need -- The dominant sequence transduction models are based on complex recurrent or convolutional neural networks in an encoder-decoder configuration. The best performing models also connect the encoder and decoder through an attention mechanism. We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely.

\cite{Chen2021_2106_01345} Decision Transformer: Reinforcement Learning via Sequence Modeling -- We introduce a framework that abstracts Reinforcement Learning (RL) as a sequence modeling problem. This allows us to draw upon the simplicity and scalability of the Transformer architecture, and associated advances in language modeling.

To this end, they release ChessBench, a large-scale dataset.

\cite{Silver2017_1712_01815} Mastering Chess and Shogi by Self-Play with a General Reinforcement Learning Algorithm -- The game of chess is the most widely-studied domain in the history of artificial intelligence. The strongest programs are based on a combination of sophisticated search techniques, domain-specific adaptations.

\section{Method} We tokenize the FEN string character-level (with reserved \texttt{[CLS]}/\texttt{[SEP]} and padding indices) and feed an 8-layer, 512-dim Transformer encoder. Two heads share the CLS representation: a policy head classifying 1{,}968 UCI moves (legal moves masked at inference and, in the masked run, in the loss) and a value head with tanh output in $[-1,1]$ trained against game results. Training uses AdamW with cosine schedule, AMP, per-epoch checkpointing with resume, and 570k/30k train/val positions from the Lichess Elite database (three months, ELO\,$>$\,2000, deduplicated). Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the overall architecture of the research pipeline.

\subsection{Graph Agent Methodology} Five LLM agents (researcher, data\_engineer, trainer, evaluator, writer) orchestrate the pipeline via \texttt{src/graph\_agents/llm\_agents.py}. Each agent combines a role prompt with a deterministic tool (the corresponding node); with \texttt{LLM\_PROVIDER} + API key it reasons via LLM, otherwise it runs deterministically. Every decision is logged to \texttt{experiments/agent\_log.jsonl} for reproducibility.
\section{Results \& Evaluation}
\begin{figure}[h]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/loss_curves.png}\caption{Four runs. Top-left: baseline (unmasked). Top-right: masked-v1. Bottom-left: value-v1 (reweighting experiment; MSE floor 0.78 = game-result label variance). Bottom-right: engine-v1 (engine targets, 71k matched positions; MSE floor 0.038 = engine-target variance). In every value regime the MSE converges to the variance floor of its targets --- the signature of a constant predictor.}\label{fig:loss_curve}\end{figure}
We evaluate four runs of our chess Transformer, varying the loss masking and value supervision while keeping the architecture fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curve}). The baseline, trained with unmasked cross-entropy (plus the value MSE), exhibits flat training dynamics: training loss decreases only marginally from 7.698 to 7.623 over 15 epochs, while validation loss oscillates between 7.61 and 8.37 with no consistent downward trend, indicating that the unmasked objective does not yield measurable generalization in this regime. Introducing legal-move masking in the loss (masked-v1) reduces the combined loss to approximately 3.74--3.75 on validation, with the cross-entropy component near 3.03 and the MSE component plateauing at roughly 0.781. The value-v1 variant, which weights the value loss $\times$3 with a $5\times$ head-specific LR, shows a higher combined loss (approximately 5.19 validation) without improving policy accuracy, showing that loss weighting alone is not the decisive factor. All runs require approximately 12.3--12.7 minutes per epoch, except the engine-supervised variant at 1.4--1.5 minutes per epoch due to its smaller matched dataset (71k positions, 12.5\% coverage).

On the game-result-supervised evaluation set, the masked-v1 checkpoint achieves a top-1 move accuracy of 15.5\% and top-3 accuracy of 36.0\%, with a value MSE of 0.719. The engine-v1 evaluation --- measured on the engine-matched held-out subset, a different and biased set (frequently analyzed, mostly balanced positions) --- reports 26.0\%/42.5\% and a value MSE of 0.0224; these numbers are \emph{not comparable} with the 15.5\%/36\% figures and we make no cross-set accuracy claim. More instructive is the value diagnosis: the engine-v1 Pearson correlation with Stockfish is $-0.0$, and direct inspection confirms a constant-predictor collapse --- on seven hand-picked positions spanning clearly winning, losing and balanced material (e.g., an extra queen for the opponent), the model outputs the same value ($+0.0321$) with a prediction standard deviation of $0.000$. The ``good'' MSE is a statistical artifact: engine targets of elite positions concentrate near zero (variance $\approx$0.03--0.04), so a constant predictor attains exactly that floor --- just as it attains 0.72 under game-result labels (variance $\approx$0.72). The two regimes were never different; MSE alone cannot distinguish a value head from a constant baseline, and prediction variance and correlation, now part of our evaluation protocol, can. We attribute the collapse to gradient competition: the cross-entropy term (scale $\approx$3.0) dominates the shared trunk's updates, while the tiny MSE residuals near the mean provide no escape signal; loss reweighting $\times$3 was insufficient, and the matched subset is both small and biased. Remedies: value training from scratch on debiased engine targets with a larger weight or separate trunk, and the spatial 8$\times$8 plane representation whose structure should ease value extraction.

\section{Attention Analysis}
The masked-CE ablation isolates the contribution of legal-move masking in the policy loss. Figure~\ref{fig:attn} shows the CLS-attention mass per square on two tactical motifs: both before (masked-v1) and after (value-v1) the value experiments, the distribution remains near-uniform (entropy 4.00--4.05 vs.\ $\ln 64 \approx 4.16$) --- tactical focus has not crystallized, consistent with a trunk shaped almost exclusively by the policy gradient. During training, masked-v1 attains its best validation top-1 of 16.8\% at epoch 0 and then settles to a plateau near 15.0--15.5\%, while validation loss remains essentially flat between 3.72 and 3.75, indicating early saturation and mild overfitting to the training distribution. By contrast, engine-v1 --- trained on the 71k matched subset --- improves from 22.0\% to 24.0\% validation top-1 within three epochs before plateauing, and its cross-entropy component decreases from 3.075 to 2.803, a stronger policy signal than the masked-v1 CE plateau near 3.03. We caution again that the engine-v1 numbers are measured on the biased matched subset and are not comparable across runs; within-run dynamics, however, suggest the matched subset is easier for the policy than the general elite distribution. We note as a limitation that all conclusions rest on 200 evaluation positions and training horizons of at most 15 epochs, so the reported differences, while consistent, should be confirmed at larger scale before strong claims are made.

\subsection{Lessons Learned: Engineering Pitfalls}
The trajectory of this project surfaced several pitfalls that we document explicitly, since they changed results or nearly did:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Silent random-init evaluation.} An evaluation run on a fresh machine silently scored a \emph{randomly initialized} model (3\% top-1) because the checkpoint directory was empty and the evaluator fell back to random weights --- producing a plausible-looking but meaningless result. Fix: the evaluator now resolves an existing checkpoint or fails loudly, and logs the checkpoint path inside the results JSON.
\item \textbf{MSE floors masquerading as learning.} The engine-target MSE (0.038) looked like an order-of-magnitude improvement until prediction variance (0.000) and Pearson correlation (0.0) revealed the constant-predictor collapse. MSE alone is insufficient for value heads; our protocol now reports all three.
\item \textbf{Unreliable training sessions.} Cloud GPU sessions (Kaggle/Colab) died mid-run repeatedly. Fix: per-epoch resumable checkpoints (model, optimizer, scheduler, AMP scaler) that made 15-epoch runs survive arbitrary interruptions.
\item \textbf{LLM writer overreach.} The writer agent occasionally produced factually wrong prose grounded on real tables (cross-set accuracy comparisons between non-comparable evaluation sets, mislabeled ablations). Fix: tables are always rendered from the JSONs by deterministic code, the LLM only narrates, and a human pass verifies every claim --- the human-in-the-loop step of the agent pipeline is not optional.
\item \textbf{Rate limits and data logistics.} arXiv API 429s (retry + cache), a 30GB download that exceeded disk quota (Lichess Elite DB at 0.3GB instead), and vocabulary shifts between runs (frequency vocabularies are regenerated per dataset and must be logged with each checkpoint).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
The masked-CE objective is the single highest-impact design decision in this study: it moved policy prediction from chance to 15.5\% top-1 / 36\% top-3 on held-out elite positions, with held-out figures matching the training-time validation band. The value head proved resistant to every intervention: game-result targets (noisy), loss reweighting and head-specific LRs (no effect), and engine-evaluated targets (constant-predictor collapse with Pearson 0.0, unmasked only by reporting prediction variance and correlation alongside MSE). The value story of this paper is therefore a methodological one: (i) MSE floors track target variance and can fake learning; (ii) diagnosing the collapse required exactly the auditability the agent pipeline provides (logged checkpoints, loud evaluator failures, per-metric JSONs); (iii) the collapse points to gradient competition with the dominant policy objective as the mechanism, and to from-scratch value training, debiased matched data, and the spatial 8$\times$8 representation as the remedies. Attention has not crystallized into tactical focus either (entropy 4.0 vs.\ $\ln 64$), consistent with a trunk shaped almost exclusively by the policy gradient. Future work: (i) value-from-scratch on engine targets with variance/correlation reporting, (ii) scale toward the ChessBench regime~\cite{Ruoss2024_2402_04494}, (iii) reintroduce search as an evaluation-time comparison rather than a training crutch. The graph-agent pipeline itself is a contribution: every number in this paper is traceable to a logged, validated agent decision.
