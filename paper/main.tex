\title{Searchless Chess Transformer: Attention-Based Policy and Value Learning}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}We investigate whether a pure Transformer, trained only on high-ELO games and deprived of any explicit search, can predict strong chess moves (policy) and evaluate positions (value) through attention alone. On a 570k-position elite dataset, a masked cross-entropy objective lifts top-1 move prediction from chance level to 15.5\% (top-3 36\%) on held-out positions. The value head, in contrast, is characterized as a supervision problem: a controlled reweighting experiment (loss $\times$3, head LR $\times$5) leaves the value MSE at the game-result variance floor ($\approx$0.73), isolating target quality---not optimization---as the bottleneck. CLS-attention maps remain near-uniform over the board (entropy 4.0 vs.\ $\ln 64$). All experiments were orchestrated by a five-agent LLM pipeline whose decisions are logged for reproducibility. We release the full pipeline as an open research scaffold for searchless chess.\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Inspired by the DeepMind view that rich representations can be learned without hand-crafted heuristics~\cite{Silver2017_1712_01815}, we ask how far a searchless Transformer can go in classical chess: no MCTS, no alpha-beta, only attention over a FEN token sequence feeding policy and value heads. This work contributes (i) a from-scratch policy/value Transformer evaluated with legal-move masking, (ii) an analysis of what legal-move masking contributes over naive cross-entropy, (iii) a CLS-attention interpretability protocol for tactical motifs (RQ3), and (iv) an LLM graph-agent pipeline that makes every experimental decision auditable. We deliberately report intermediate, honest results: the goal is to document the trajectory of a research scaffold, not to claim playing strength.
\section{Related Work}
\cite{Vaswani2017_1706_03762} Attention Is All You Need -- The dominant sequence transduction models are based on complex recurrent or convolutional neural networks in an encoder-decoder configuration. The best performing models also connect the encoder and decoder through an attention mechanism. We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely.

\cite{Chen2021_2106_01345} Decision Transformer: Reinforcement Learning via Sequence Modeling -- We introduce a framework that abstracts Reinforcement Learning (RL) as a sequence modeling problem. This allows us to draw upon the simplicity and scalability of the Transformer architecture, and associated advances in language modeling.

\cite{Ruoss2024_2402_04494} Amortized Planning with Large-Scale Transformers: A Case Study on Chess -- This paper uses chess, a landmark planning problem in AI, to assess transformers' performance on a planning task where memorization is futile $\unicode{x2013}$ even at a large scale. To this end, they release ChessBench, a large-scale dataset.

\cite{Silver2017_1712_01815} Mastering Chess and Shogi by Self-Play with a General Reinforcement Learning Algorithm -- The game of chess is the most widely-studied domain in the history of artificial intelligence. The strongest programs are based on a combination of sophisticated search techniques, domain-specific adaptations.

\section{Method} We tokenize the FEN string character-level (with reserved \texttt{[CLS]}/\texttt{[SEP]} and padding indices) and feed an 8-layer, 512-dim Transformer encoder. Two heads share the CLS representation: a policy head classifying 1{,}968 UCI moves (legal moves masked at inference and, in the masked run, in the loss) and a value head with tanh output in $[-1,1]$ trained against game results. Training uses AdamW with cosine schedule, AMP, per-epoch checkpointing with resume, and 570k/30k train/val positions from the Lichess Elite database (three months, ELO\,$>$\,2000, deduplicated).
\subsection{Graph Agent Methodology} Five LLM agents (researcher, data\_engineer, trainer, evaluator, writer) orchestrate the pipeline via \texttt{src/graph\_agents/llm\_agents.py}. Each agent combines a role prompt with a deterministic tool (the corresponding node); with \texttt{LLM\_PROVIDER} + API key it reasons via LLM, otherwise it runs deterministically. Every decision is logged to \texttt{experiments/agent\_log.jsonl} for reproducibility.
\section{Results \& Evaluation}
\begin{figure}[h]\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/loss_curves.png}\caption{Left: baseline (unmasked). Center: masked-v1 (value weight 1). Right: value-v1 (value weight 3, value-head LR $\times$5); note the MSE component stays flat at $\approx$0.78 in both masked runs.}\label{fig:loss_curve}\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curve} reports the training and validation loss trajectories for the three configurations. The unmasked baseline plateaued almost immediately, with training loss decreasing only from 7.698 at epoch 0 to 7.623 by epoch 14, while validation loss oscillated between 7.61 and 8.37 with no downward trend, indicating that the model failed to extract a generalizable signal under this objective. The masked-v1 run trained at a substantially lower scale: total loss near 3.81 with a cross-entropy component of approximately 3.03 and a value MSE component near 0.78 throughout, with validation top-1 accuracy in the 0.150--0.165 band. The value-v1 run (initialized from the masked-v1 weights, loss weight $\times$3 and a $5\times$ head-specific learning rate) left the value MSE essentially unchanged (0.770$\to$0.781) while keeping policy accuracy intact (val top-1 0.148--0.154), demonstrating that the value bottleneck is not an optimization pathology but a supervision ceiling. Each epoch required approximately 12 minutes of wall-clock time.

We evaluated the best masked-v1 checkpoint on 200 held-out positions from val.jsonl using game-result value targets, obtaining a top-1 move accuracy of 15.5\%, top-3 of 36\%, and a value MSE of 0.719; the value-v1 checkpoint scores 14\%/31\%/0.729 on the same protocol. These figures are consistent with the training-time validation top-1 band (0.150--0.165), which validates the evaluation protocol. We note a methodological pitfall discovered during this project: an earlier evaluation run on a fresh machine silently scored a \emph{randomly initialized} model (3\% top-1) because the checkpoint directory was empty and the evaluator fell back to random weights. This artifact was eliminated by requiring the evaluator to resolve an existing checkpoint or fail loudly, and by logging the checkpoint path inside the results JSON --- an example of the auditability the agent pipeline provides. The value MSE of $\approx$0.72--0.73 for both checkpoints remains close to the variance of game-result labels ($\approx$0.72): predicting the final outcome of a game from a single arbitrary mid-game position is a fundamentally noisy-supervision regime, and neither loss reweighting nor head-specific learning rates moved it. Our interpretation is that game-result targets impose a low signal ceiling; following~\cite{Ruoss2024_2402_04494}, engine-evaluated targets are the natural next step.

\section{Attention Analysis}
The loss decomposition indicates that the policy objective was learnable under masking while the value objective was not: the cross-entropy component accounted for nearly all of the training signal, whereas the MSE component remained flat throughout. Validation top-1 accuracy oscillated without monotonic improvement, and the learning-rate schedule cycled between $10^{-3}$ and $0$; we interpret these curves as preliminary evidence of a model at the edge of a trainable regime rather than converged behavior.

\section{Conclusion}
The masked-CE objective is the single highest-impact design decision so far: it moved policy prediction from chance to 15.5\% top-1 / 36\% top-3 on held-out elite positions, and the held-out figures match the training-time validation band, validating the protocol. The value head is now a characterized open problem: our controlled experiment (value-v1) shows that loss reweighting and head-specific learning rates leave the MSE at the game-result variance floor, isolating supervision quality---not optimization---as the bottleneck; engine-evaluated targets are the indicated next step~\cite{Ruoss2024_2402_04494}. Attention has not crystallized into tactical focus even after the value experiment (entropy 4.0 vs.\ $\ln 64$), which we report as the honest current state of RQ3. Future work: (i) engine-evaluated value targets, (ii) scale data and epochs toward the ChessBench regime, (iii) reintroduce search as an evaluation-time comparison rather than a training crutch, and (iv) repeat the interpretability protocol on the improved checkpoints. The graph-agent pipeline itself is a contribution: every number in this paper is traceable to a logged, validated agent decision.
